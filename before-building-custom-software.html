What Businesses Should Consider Before Building Custom Software

Build or buy, defining scope and ownership, integrations and data, maintenance and total cost, and how to choose a development partner: the questions to settle before commissioning custom software.

Buddhi Labs Team

Published 4 October 2026 · Software Development

Custom software is the right answer for some problems and an expensive answer for others. The organizations that get good results are usually the ones that settled a handful of questions before any code was written. This article lists those questions, in the order we recommend working through them.

Build or buy?

Start by asking whether the problem is specific to your organization. Payroll, accounting, email and, in most cases, HR management are solved well by existing products, and a subscription will be cheaper and faster than a build. Custom software earns its cost when the workflow itself is your competitive advantage, when no product fits the way you operate, or when several existing systems need to be connected in a way no vendor supports.

A useful test: if you can describe the need using the features of a product you have already seen, buy. If you keep saying "like that, but…", a build may be justified.

Define scope and ownership

  • Who owns the outcome? One person in your organization must be able to make decisions about priorities and trade-offs. Projects without that person stall in committee.
  • What is the smallest useful version? Decide what must work on day one and what can follow. A first release that three people use every day teaches more than a year-long plan.
  • What is explicitly out of scope? Writing this down prevents the slow expansion that breaks timelines.

Integrations and data

Most custom systems fail or succeed on their edges: the systems they import from and export to. List every data source the software needs, who controls it, and in what format it is available today. Decide early where the master copy of each kind of data lives. Plan the migration of existing records as a task with its own owner and timeline, not as something that happens at launch.

Maintenance and total cost

The build is the smallest part of the cost of software that lasts. Hosting, security updates, dependency upgrades, user support and small changes continue for as long as the system is in use. Ask any partner how maintenance is priced, what documentation you will receive, and whether you could hand the code to another team if you needed to. Clean code, written documentation and a clear handover are not extras; they are the difference between an asset and a liability.

Choosing a partner

Look for a partner who asks about your workflow before proposing technology, who agrees scope, timeline and pricing in writing, who shows working software regularly rather than reporting progress, and who will support the system after launch.
